    The arithmetic behind everything

    Every chapter uses the same eight tools. Each is shown here with a real number from the app, so you can check it on a phone calculator. If a later formula looks scary, come back to this page: it is always one of these eight.

    1 · Decimals and percentages

    A chance is a number between 0 and 1. Multiply by 100 to read it as a percentage.

    0.6726 × 100 = 67.26%   (the market's chance that Monaco beat Auxerre on 21 Nov)

    2 · The mean (average)

    mean = x1 + x2 + … + xnnAdd them up, divide by how many there are. Written short: (1/n) Σ x. The Σ sign just means "add up all of".

    3 · The weighted average

    Same idea, but some numbers count more. The weights add up to 1.

    weighted mean = w1x1 + w2x2 + w3x3    with w1 + w2 + w3 = 1

    4 · Profit, return and break-even

    win: profit = stake × (o − 1)    lose: profit = −stake    ROI = total profittotal staked    break-even win rate = 1o
    ₹100 on Monaco @ 1.42 → win: 100 × 0.42 = +₹42 · lose: −₹100
    4 such bets, 3 won: 3 × 42 − 100 = +₹26 on ₹400 staked → ROI = 26 ÷ 400 = +6.5%
    to break even at 1.42 you must win 1 ÷ 1.42 = 70.4% of the time

    5 · e, powers and the natural log (ln)

    e = 2.71828… is just a fixed number, like π. ex means e multiplied by itself x times (it works for fractions too). ln is the undo button: ln(ex) = x.

    xexand back
    01ln 1 = 0
    0.6932ln 2 = 0.693
    12.718ln 2.718 = 1
    −0.6930.5ln 0.5 = −0.693

    Two rules you will use: ln(a/b) = ln a − ln b, and e−x = 1/ex.

    ln(0.6726 ÷ 0.3274) = ln(2.0544) = 0.720  (Monaco's market chance on the log-odds scale, Chapter 2)

    6 · Spread: the standard deviation

    s = √Σ (x − mean)²n     z-score = x − meansHow far numbers typically sit from their mean. The z-score says "how many typical steps from normal" a value is: the model uses it to put every feature on the same scale.

    7 · tanh: a squash into −1 … +1

    tanh(x) = ex − e−xex + e−xNear 0 it is almost x itself; big numbers get flattened towards ±1. tanh(0.5) = 0.462, tanh(1) = 0.762, tanh(2) = 0.964. Used for the team grades and the news tone, so one extreme value can't run off the scale.
    PSG's own edge 0.0117 ÷ 0.015 = 0.78 → tanh(0.78) = 0.653 → 50 + 50 × 0.653 = 82.6 ≈ 83 out of 100 (Chapter 20)

    8 · Halving: the half-life

    weight = 0.5age ÷ hAfter h days a thing counts half, after 2h a quarter. News uses h = 4 days.
    age 0 → 1 · age 4 → 0.5 · age 8 → 0.25 · age 13.6 days → 0.53.4 = 0.095

    Chapter 1 · Foundations

    Odds are chances in disguise

    Decimal odds say how much you get back per ₹1 staked if the bet wins. Odds of 1.80 return ₹180 on ₹100. Turn them around and they become the bookmaker's view of the chance.

    qi = 1oi oi is the decimal price of outcome i (home, draw, away); qi is its implied chance.

    The three implied chances add up to more than 100%. The extra is the overround, the bookmaker's margin. To get fair chances we divide it out:

    overround = qH + qD + qA − 1     mi = qiqH + qD + qA mi is the margin-free ("no-vig") chance. Every model on this page starts from m for the side you back.

    Try it: Brighton v Brentford, 8 Nov, closing average

    Why the closing price is hard to beat

    The closing price is the last one before kick-off. By then it carries the line-ups, the news and the money of the sharpest bettors. Across 9,288 test bets, backing the side at its closing price lost , roughly the margin. A model is useful only if it finds where this price is wrong.

    Chapter 2 · Foundations

    Log-odds: the scale models think on

    A chance lives between 0 and 1, which makes adding effects awkward: +5 points on a 97% favourite would break 100%. So models work on log-odds (also called the logit), which runs from −∞ to +∞, and turn the answer back into a chance at the end with the sigmoid.

    logit(p) = ln p1 − p      σ(z) = 11 + e−z σ is the inverse of logit: σ(logit(p)) = p. A 50% chance is log-odds 0; 73% is +1; 88% is +2.

    Try it

    log-odds
    +0.10 on log-odds gives

    The same +0.10 log-odds moves a 50% chance by about 2.5 points but a 95% chance by only 0.5. That is the slope of the sigmoid: dp/dz = p(1 − p).

    Remember Δp ≈ Δz · p(1 − p). At a typical 55% chance, p(1 − p) ≈ 0.25, so a weight of 0.04 on the log-odds scale is about 1 percentage point.
    Chapter 3 · Foundations

    The data: one bet a match

    Every league match in England, Spain, Italy, Germany and France from 2016/17 to now with a closing price: matches. For each one we back a side: your team if one of your ₹ teams plays ( matches), otherwise the favourite. The target is simple:

    y = 1 if the backed side wins, 0 for a draw or a loss

    Each match gets twelve features, all computed only from what was known before kick-off. Matches in cups are not in the files, so form counts league games only.

    Two features need the price

    Raw form misleads: winning 8 of 12 at average odds of 1.30 is expected, at 2.20 it is remarkable. So two features measure results beyond what the prices expected:

    xPts = 3 · mwin + 1 · mdraw     pvp12 = 112 Σ (points − xPts) Expected points from the no-vig chances of that match, summed over the last 12.
    xGD = · (mhome − maway)     gdvp6 = 16 Σ (goal difference − xGD) The line was fitted on all 29,393 matches: a side the market makes 30 points likelier to win than its opponent is expected to win by about 0.75 goals.

    Standardising

    League position runs 1–20 while share of shots runs 0–1. To make weights comparable, each feature is rescaled to mean 0 and spread 1 using the training data:

    x̃ = x − μsμ and s are the training mean and standard deviation (in the table). So a weight is "the effect of one typical step".

    Chapter 4 · Foundations

    What makes a model good

    Log-loss: the score we minimise

    A model gives a chance p; the match gives y. Log-loss punishes confident mistakes hard.

    LogLoss = −1N Σ [ y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p) ] Lower is better. Saying 50% on everything scores ln 2 = 0.693. The market scores on the test seasons.

    Worked on your five, with the market's chance

    Brier score is the other common one: (1/N) Σ (p − y)², the mean squared error of the chance.

    Calibration

    Group the bets by predicted chance. A calibrated model's 70% bets win 70% of the time. Chapter 8 shows a model that fails this.

    Overfitting and walk-forward testing

    A flexible model can memorise the past, noise included. The only fair test is on seasons it has never seen. We train on every season before, test on the next, then move forward one season:

    From chance to a bet

    A bet has positive expected value when our chance times the price is above 1:

    EV = p · o − 1 > 0  ⇔  p > 1oEvery model's ROI on this page is: bet ₹100 when p · o > 1, skip otherwise.

    Chapter 5 · Model

    Logistic regression, starting from the market

    The idea. Take the market's log-odds as the starting point and let each feature push it up or down by a fixed amount per unit. Add up, then squash back into a chance.

    z = logit(m) + b + Σj wj x̃j     p = σ(z) logit(m) is the offset: fixed, not learned. b is the intercept, wj the weight of feature j. With every weight 0 the model is the market.

    How it learns

    It picks the weights that make the observed results most likely: maximum likelihood, which is the same as minimising log-loss. Add a penalty on large weights (L2 regularisation) so noise can't buy big weights:

    L(w) = −Σn [ yn ln pn + (1 − yn) ln(1 − pn) ] + λ2 ‖w‖²

    The gradient has a beautifully simple form, the features times the errors:

    ∇L = −XT(y − p) + λw     H = XT diag(p(1 − p)) X + λI We used Newton's method: w ← w − H−1∇L. It converges in a handful of steps because log-loss is convex: one valley, no traps. λ = 2 here.

    Chapter 6 · Model

    GAM: logistic regression that can bend

    The idea. Logistic regression says every extra loss counts the same. Real effects bend: one loss in five may mean nothing while three means a crisis. A generalised additive model replaces each straight line with a flexible curve fj, but still adds them up, so each curve stays readable.

    z = logit(m) + b + Σj fj(x̃j)     fj(x) = wj0 x + Σk wjk · max(0, x − κk) Each max(0, x − κ) is a hinge that switches on past a knot κ, letting the slope change there. Knots at each feature's 25th, 50th and 75th percentile. Fitted exactly like Chapter 5, with a stronger penalty (λ = 6) because there are 4× as many weights.

    Chapter 7 · Model

    Decision trees, then gradient boosting

    One tree

    A tree asks yes/no questions ("losses in last 5 ≤ 2?") and puts each match in a leaf. At each step it tries every feature and every cut, and keeps the one that best separates the outcomes. For a regression tree, "best" means the biggest fall in squared error:

    gain = Σparent(g − ḡ)² − [ Σleft(g − ḡL)² + Σright(g − ḡR)² ] For classification trees you will also hear Gini impurity, 1 − Σ pc². Boosting uses regression trees on the errors, as below.

    Boosting: many small trees fixing each other

    Start from the market's log-odds. Fit a small tree to what the current model gets wrong, add a little of it, repeat:

    F0 = logit(m)    gn = yn − pn    hn = pn(1 − pn)
    leaf value v = Σleaf gnΣleaf hn + λ    Ft = Ft−1 + η · vleaf(x) g is the gradient (the error), h the Hessian (how sure we were). The leaf value is a Newton step, the same maths as Chapter 5 done one leaf at a time. η = 0.05 is the learning rate; trees are 2 levels deep with at least 300 matches per leaf and λ = 10. This is what LightGBM and XGBoost do, with faster split-finding.

    How many trees? Too few underfit, too many memorise. We held out the last training season and stopped where its log-loss was lowest: trees for the latest model. In 2022/23 the best number was 1, meaning the trees found nothing that held up.

    Chapter 8 · Model

    Random forest: many deep trees voting

    The idea. Grow hundreds of deep trees, each on a random resample of the matches (bagging) and each split choosing from a random subset of features. Their mistakes differ, so the average is steadier than any one tree.

    p(x) = 1T Σt=1T pt(x) T = 300 trees, at least 40 matches per leaf, √12 ≈ 3 features tried per split. Averaging cuts the variance of one tree by up to a factor of T when the trees are uncorrelated.

    A forest cannot start from the market's log-odds: it has no offset. So the market's chance goes in as just another feature, and the forest has to relearn the bookmaker from scratch. It leans on it heavily:

    Chapter 9 · Model

    Neural network: layers of tiny regressions

    The idea. A neuron is a logistic regression with a different squash. Stack a layer of them, feed their outputs into one more, and the network can learn shapes no single formula was told about.

    h = ReLU(W1T x + b1)    p = σ(W2T h + b2)    ReLU(u) = max(0, u) Trained by backpropagation: the chain rule carries the error back through each layer, e.g. ∂L/∂W2 = h(p − y), and the optimiser (Adam) steps every weight downhill.

    Chapter 10 · Model

    Bayesian shrinkage: grading teams honestly

    The idea. To grade a team, measure how often it beat its price: r = mean(y − m) over its matches. But a team on +5% over 340 matches may just be lucky. Shrinkage pulls each team towards zero by how much of its number is likely noise.

    rteam ~ Normal(θteam, σ²/n)    θteam ~ Normal(0, τ²)
    θ̂ = r · nn + σ²/τ² σ² ≈ average m(1 − m), the noise of one match. τ² is how much teams truly differ, estimated from the data: τ² = Var(team r) − mean(σ²/n), the spread we see minus the spread luck alone would make. This is empirical Bayes.

    Is +3.4% real?

    Each ₹100 bet at odds o has a standard deviation of about ₹100·√(o − 1). At odds near 2 that is ₹100 a bet, so over N bets:

    SE(ROI) ≈ √(o − 1)√N ≈ 1√1915 ≈ 2.3%So +3.4% is about 1.5 standard errors from zero: encouraging, positive in 5 of 6 seasons, not yet proof.

    Chapter 14 · Part 2

    The favourite–longshot bias

    The market is not equally wrong everywhere. Group all tested sides by the market's chance and compare with how often they really won:

    Read it: longshots (under 30%) win less often than their price says, and ₹100 on every one of them loses 11–20%. Favourites win a touch more often. Bookmakers put most of their margin on longshots, because people love backing them.

    The fix: let the model stretch the market

    In Part 1 the market's log-odds were only the starting point. Now they are also a feature, with their own learned weight:

    z = logit(m) + b + wm · logit(m) − μs + (other features)
    Why the app only backs favourites. With both sides as rows, the first fit found "value" on underdogs that lost about 9% when tested. Even after this fix, underdog value didn't hold up, so a pick must be the favourite and have chance × price above 1.

      Learning the weights, by hand

      Where do numbers like 0.147 come from? The computer tries a weight, sees how wrong the chances are, and corrects, a few times. Here is the same method on six made-up matches and one feature, so every number fits on screen.

      pn = σ(logit(mn) + w · xn)
      slope: g = Σ xn(yn − pn) − λw    curvature: H = Σ pn(1 − pn)xn² + λ    new w = w + gH y − p is the miss on each match. If the matches with a high x won more than predicted, g is positive and w goes up. H says how fast the slope changes, so the step is the right size (Newton's method). λ is the penalty that keeps w small unless the data insist. Here λ = .

      In the app it is the same thing with 13 weights at once (an intercept plus 12 features), 36,312 rows and λ = 2: g becomes a list (the gradient), H a 13 × 13 table (the Hessian), and "divide by H" becomes solving a set of equations. It stops when no weight moves by more than 0.000000001, usually within 6 rounds.

      Chapter 20 · Part 2

      The team grade, 0 to 100

      Every club in the five leagues gets a grade for "how good is it to back with this model". It is a weighted average (Chapter 0, tool 3) of three parts, each turned into 0–100 first:

      grade = 0.5 × edge score + 0.2 × record score + 0.3 × backable score

      Part A · Own edge (50%)

      For each of the club's last 20 matches: the model's chance minus the market's, less the usual lift the model gives any side at that price. That lift is the bias fix from Chapter 14; without removing it, every favourite would look gifted.

      Part B · Value record (20%)

      Its value bets as favourite over the last two seasons: P/L at ₹100 each, then shrunk for small samples. With n bets the return is multiplied by n ÷ (n + 40), so 7 bets keep only 15% of their return and 40 bets keep half.

      Part C · Backable (30%)

      Put together, then your stars

      shown score = 0.6 × model grade + 0.4 × (20 × your stars)Each star is worth 20 points, so 5★ = 100. No stars: the model grade alone.

      Chapter 21 · Part 2

      Live picks, and how long until we know

      The backtest can flatter. The real test is bets recorded before the matches, which can't be changed afterwards. Every 20 minutes the app:

      1. Prices the next 3 daysThe week's market average, or ESPN's sportsbook line until that is out; ESPN's line and the starting XIs from 3 hours before kick-off.
      2. Runs the modelWith line-ups once both XIs are out (Ch 18), otherwise without.
      3. Keeps the value favouritesFavourite, and chance × price > 1. A pick that stops being value before kick-off is dropped.
      4. Freezes at kick-offNothing can change after the whistle.
      5. Settles at ₹100At the closing average once the weekly file has it (as the backtest did), else at the price picked.

      Recorded so far

      How many bets before we can trust the number?

      Each ₹100 bet at odds o swings by about ₹100 × √(o − 1). Averaging N bets shrinks the swing by √N. To be fairly sure (2 standard errors) an edge is real:

      SE = √(o − 1)√N     need ROI > 2 × SE  ⇔  N > 4(o − 1)ROI²
